## Day one without the map

You start on a Monday. The place already has its heroes, its scars and its in-jokes, and you don't get any of them yet. Someone mentions "the old way" and the room goes quiet for half a second. Your first ticket triage looks fine on paper. Your first all-hands doesn't land the way you practised it.

That's normal. You're an outsider leader. You were hired for what you did somewhere else and dropped into a team that already has its own version of the truth.

The first 90 days aren't for transforming anything. They're for earning the right to change things without becoming the person who "doesn't get how we work here."

## Trust isn't built at a town hall

I've spent 13 years moving between technical support, systems administration, network engineering and operations. Each move meant walking into a team that already had its own way of doing things, and I had to learn that way before I'd earned the right to change any of it. Now I lead a service desk spread across EMEA and APAC, and I watch new starters and new managers go through the same thing every year.

Process posts will tell you to map the value stream. That's fine, but it's only half the picture. What earned me trust early was simpler:

- Listen where the work happens. Sit with L1 and L2 on real tickets, not a curated demo. Ask what they try three times before they escalate, and what they never write down because "everyone knows."
- Admit what you don't know. "I need the map" beats fake certainty. People forgive a new leader for not knowing things. They don't forgive one who rewrites a runbook before living through a single Monday with it.
- Protect whoever tells you the hard stuff. If the first honest message about a broken VIP path gets punished, all you'll hear from then on is green.
- Fix something that removes pain, not something that proves you've arrived. That might be a clearer handoff note, a named owner for a recurring failure, or screenshots that match the console people actually use. Keep it small, visible and useful to the desk.

Charisma won't cover for a lack of credibility. Credibility is a pile of small promises kept in front of the people who pick up the queue at 2am.

## What outsiders get wrong

Three traps come up fast.

**The reformer.** You bring a framework from your last job and start renaming queues in week two. What the team hears is that their history doesn't count. That pushback isn't politics. It's memory protecting itself.

**The tourist.** You spend 90 days in leadership meetings and vendor calls and never sit on the desk. You leave with a strategy deck and no allies for the first Sev1 that needs a calm voice.

**The silent one.** You wait until you "fully understand" before saying anything, but you never fully understand. Meanwhile the team decides you've got no point of view, or that you're quietly building a case for a restructure.

What works is quieter than all three: be curious, with a spine. Learn how things really run. Hold a few non-negotiables from day one and earn the rest. Mine are:

- no pretending a green dashboard means everything's fine
- no shadow VIP path
- RCAs that actually ship a change

## The team already has leaders

Some of the most trusted people won't have your job title. There's the analyst who knows which vendor actually picks up, and the shift lead who can calm a VIP without opening a side channel. There's also the engineer who'll tell you the real story of the platform without polishing it.

Find them in your first two weeks. Ask who people call when the process fails. That informal map will be more accurate than the org chart for your whole first quarter.

Then do the thing a lot of leaders skip, and credit them publicly when a fix works. An outsider who takes the win doesn't get a second honest conversation.

## A rough shape for 90 days

This isn't a programme. It's a rough order of things.

**Days 1–30: map and listen.** Find out who owns what when it breaks and where tickets go to die. Find out where VIPs go when the main path feels slow, and which metrics look green but feel wrong. Write it down for yourself, and hold off on the transformation memo.

**Days 31–60: fix one old problem with the people who live with it.** Pick something the desk already hates and see it through with them. Make the change visible, and let the story be theirs as much as yours.

**Days 61–90: agree what comes next.** By now you've earned enough trust to talk about the bigger things:

- recurring issues
- knowledge that survives the night shift
- why users are still angry when every SLA is green

People will hear "we" instead of "the new person."

Jump to day-90 language on day five and you'll get polite nods and quiet workarounds.

## This week's challenge

If you're new in a role, or about to be, don't open with a reorg slide.

Pick one place where things already hurt. Sit with the people who deal with it. Ask them what a good outsider would protect and what a bad one would break. Then keep one small promise in the next ten working days. It could be a clearer path, a named owner, or a runbook that matches reality.

That's how you stop being "the new leader" and become someone the night shift still calls when it matters.
