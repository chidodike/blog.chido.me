Most of the year, the service desk is judged on speed. How fast the queue moves, whether the SLA is green, whether the person on the other end felt looked after. Then an audit shows up, and the question changes. SOC 2, ISO 27001, and Cyber Essentials are different certificates, but they all want the same thing from the people who run the desk: proof of who joined, who left, and who can still sign in.

That proof is not a policy and it is not a dashboard. It is the ticket that created an account, the person who approved the access, the device that walked out the door, and the leaver step that actually turned the sign-in off. When those pieces sit in different tools, or in one person’s spreadsheet, the desk is the one left explaining a control it does not fully hold. That is the week this is about. Not how to pass an audit. What it feels like when the questionnaire lands on the people who can show the work.

It is Wednesday, 16:10. Someone from the audit firm has sent a list that looks polite and is not. Joiners in the last ninety days. Leavers. Who approved access. Which devices those people used on day one. Evidence, not a policy link. The person who owns the master spreadsheet is on a train with a dying laptop. The service desk has the tickets. Security has the control wording. Neither of them, right now, can tell the story without that file.

That is the week. Not the badge. The week.

SOC 2, ISO 27001, and Cyber Essentials are different certificates. On the desk they ask the same question. Can you show, for a real person, how they got an account, a device, and a set of rights, and how those things stop when they leave? A green SLA pack does not answer it. Volume, speed, and a healthy CSAT say the queue is moving. They do not say the joiner from March still has access to a system they used for six weeks and then never touched again.

## The desk is the evidence

I have sat in the reviews where the slide says access is reviewed quarterly and the room goes quiet when someone asks for one name. The control is real on paper. The proof is a conversation between three teams who each hold a third of it.

Joiners, movers, and leavers are not an HR workflow with an IT footnote. They are the audit. The ticket that created the account, the manager who approved the group, the device that was handed over, the leaver checklist that actually disabled the sign-in. If those live in four tools and one person's memory, the certificate is a story you hope nobody interrupts.

Cyber Essentials is the smallest of the three and it still wants this. Who has admin. Whether leavers are gone. Whether the devices you claim are yours are the ones people actually use. ISO 27001 will ask how you know the process keeps happening after the audit team goes home. SOC 2 will ask for the population and the sample, then sit with you while you open the ticket. Different badges. Same Tuesday.

What fails is not usually the absence of a process. It is the process that only works when its author is in the building. The spreadsheet with twelve tabs. The "we pull that from the console if they ask." The leaver that was done in Teams and never became a ticket. The joiner whose extra access was a favour, so it is not on the form the auditor is holding.

## What you can actually show

You do not need a new audit tool for this week. You need one population you can narrate without the spreadsheet owner.

Take joiners from the last ninety days. Not the whole company. Not every system. One list.

For each person, the desk should be able to say, without a side call:

- the request that started it, and who approved it
- the account that was created, and the access that was actually granted, including the extra bit that was not on the form
- the device they left with, and who owns it in the register
- what would happen to all of that if they resigned on Friday

If any of those needs the person who is on the train, that is the finding. Write it down before the auditor does.

The green pack can stay in the leadership meeting. It is not what you put on the table when someone asks for a name. The thing you put on the table is a ticket, an approval, a device record, and a leaver path that still works when the usual person is offline.

Pick that list this week. Ten joiners is enough. If the desk can explain them, you have evidence. If it cannot, you have the work, and it is better to find it on Wednesday than in the sample.
