Every service desk I've worked with has a self-service portal. Very few have one that people choose over picking up the phone.

The usual story goes like this. We build the portal, fill it with forms and knowledge articles, announce it, and watch adoption flatten at a number nobody wants to say out loud. Then someone says the users just need training. They don't. The portal is telling us something.

Think about what a person actually does when their laptop won't connect to the [VPN](/posts/vpn-is-the-ticket/) at 8:55 before a meeting. They don't browse a catalogue. They don't know whether this is an "Incident", a "Request" or a "Network Issue". They want the problem gone, or at least a straight answer. If the first screen asks them to classify their own problem in our language, they close the tab and message the person they know.

That's the friction I'd name: self-service often makes people do the triage for us.

What I've seen work is boring and specific:

1. Start from the top ten reasons people contact the desk, not from the service catalogue. Build those ten journeys properly, in the words users use. ["Can't sign in"](/posts/redefining-day-one/) beats "Identity access request".
2. Make the fix the first thing on the page. If an article needs scrolling past a policy paragraph to reach step one, it isn't a help article, it's a notice.
3. Let the portal finish the job where it can. Password reset, software install, shared mailbox access: if it still ends in a ticket and a wait, people learn it's just a slower phone call.
4. When self-service fails, make the hand-off warm. The ticket should carry what they already tried, so nobody asks them to start again. That moment decides whether they ever come back.
5. [Measure deflection](/posts/green-slas/) honestly. A ticket that never got raised because the person gave up is not a win. Watch repeat contacts and "was this useful" on the article, not just portal visits.

The test I like: take someone new, give them a real problem, and don't help. If they solve it in two minutes, you have self-service. If they ask where to click, you have a website.

Good self-service isn't about reducing calls. It's about respecting the fact that most people would rather fix it themselves, if we stop making that harder than ringing us.
